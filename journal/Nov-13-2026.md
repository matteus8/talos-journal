### The Network Dilemma: Hotspot vs. Dedicated Subnet
At first I had this clever thought: what if I just connect the Surface 3, the Pi 5, and my Mac to my phone's hotspot? In theory, it creates a completely segregated network away from the rest of my home Wi-Fi traffic.

Turns out it's not great cause:
1. The Dilemma: My hotspot has "AP Client Isolation" enabled by default, which is actually great for everyday security. Essentially, connected devices can only talk out to the internet, but can't talk to each other. In this setup, the Surface 3 worker has to talk directly to the Pi 5 control plane on port 6443, and my Mac has to reach both on port 50000. On my phone hotspot, this peer-to-peer traffic will likely get dropped. 
2. My hotspot will hand out random DHCP addresses rather than getting the needed static IPs.

So the plan is simply to stick with the local home subnet (`192.168.1.0/24`), carving out dedicated static IPs outside the typical router lease pool:
- `192.168.1.100` for the Pi 5 control plane
  - this gives me the block x.x.1.100 - x.x.1.105 if I want to expand to an HA etcd cluster (3-5 master nodes)
- `192.168.1.106` for the Surface 3 worker
  - this leaves x.x.1.106 through x.x.1.254 for worker nodes (~148 worker nodes in our /24 subnet)
> NOTE: I understand in Defense Networking, these ranges will likley be in the 10.x.x.x range and be given to the tech configruing Talos OS. Which shows some of the power of these config files, we can change the IP's qickly.

## Side quests (archived thoughts)
Just some random side stuff that popped up while trying to deploy Talos

### The hardware pains
1. After dusting off this old tablet (which I thought was a Surface Pro 2 or Pro 3) - it turns out, it was just a non-pro **Surface 3**, and looking at the specs... with [2gb of RAM](https://docs.siderolabs.com/talos/v1.14/getting-started/system-requirements).... AAAAAGGHHHHH.... after a short crash out, I composed myself.
    - See [Surface 3 Specs](./Nov-13-2026/surface-3-specs.jpg)
2. Here is why that spec's really frustrated me:
- The RAM (2.00 GB): Sidero's docs say 2GB is the bare minimum for Talos, but that is thin for a control plane. With `etcd` taking ~500MB and `kube-apiserver` taking ~700MB+, my 2GB master node might hit OOO and destroy the Kubernetes API.
- The Disk (eMMC vs SSD): Surface 3 does not have an mSATA or NVMe SSD. It has soldered eMMC flash memory, which registers in Linux as `/dev/mmcblk0` (not `/dev/sda`). If I had blindly set `machine.install.disk: /dev/sda` in my Talos config, it would have tried to wipe my bootable USB stick instead of the internal drive.
- The CPU: The *Intel Atom x7-Z8700* is a *2015 Cherry Trail quad-core* running at *1.6GHz*. My Raspberry Pi 5 runs a modern *Broadcom BCM2712 quad-core Cortex-A76* at *2.4GHz* with **4.0 GiB of RAM and hardware crypto** (`aes`, `sha1`, `sha2`). The Pi 5 runs circles around this little tablet.
- Secure Boot is ON: Just noting the UEFI settings show Secure Boot is active. Before Talos can boot from USB, I have to turn Secure Boot off in UEFI.
- The TPM 2.0 Silver Lining: Looking closely at the specs under `PCR7 Configuration`, the Surface 3 actually has an onboard **TPM 2.0** chip (Intel PTT)! While the Pi 5 doesn't have an onboard TPM out-of-the-box, having TPM 2.0 on the Surface 3 means we can actually demo true hardware-sealed LUKS disk encryption on our worker node to mimic the THOR edge security architecture.

3. The Pivot: Swapping Control Plane & Worker
- After pulling the Pi 5 specs (`hostnamectl`, `free -h`, `cat /proc/cpuinfo`), it confirmed my Pi 5 has **4.0 GiB of RAM** and quad Cortex-A76 cores with hardware crypto acceleration. 
- Because of this, swapping the roles is a no-brainer:
  - **Control Plane $\rightarrow$ Raspberry Pi 5 (`192.168.1.100`):** 4GB of RAM gives `etcd` and `kube-apiserver` the breathing room they need (avoiding the 2GB OOM trap), and the hardware crypto engine handles Talos's continuous mTLS handshakes in silicon without slowing down.
  - **Worker Node $\rightarrow$ Surface 3 (`192.168.1.106`):** 2GB of RAM and an Atom CPU are plenty for a lightweight edge worker running simple consumer pods or sensor simulators. And we target `/dev/mmcblk0` for the internal eMMC install.

4. **Hardware Comparison Reference/Table**
Quick cheat sheet comparing both bare-metal edge nodes for our lab:

| Metric | Raspberry Pi 5 (**Control Plane**) | Surface 3 (**Worker Node**) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Role** | Control Plane (Master) | Worker Node |
| **Target IP** | `192.168.1.100` | `192.168.1.106` |
| **Architecture** | ARM64 (`aarch64`) | x86_64 (`amd64`) |
| **CPU** | Broadcom BCM2712 (4x Cortex-A76 @ 2.4GHz) | Intel Atom x7-Z8700 (4 cores @ 1.6GHz) |
| **Hardware Crypto** | Yes (`aes`, `pmull`, `sha1`, `sha2`, `crc32`) | Older x86 AES-NI |
| **RAM** | 4.0 GiB LPDDR4X | 2.0 GiB |
| **Internal Storage** | 64 GB MicroSD (`/dev/mmcblk0`) | 64 GB eMMC (`/dev/mmcblk0`) |
| **Boot Media** | MicroSD card (flashed with Pi schematic) | USB Stick (installer targeting `/dev/mmcblk0`) |
| **Firmware / Boot** | RPi EEPROM Bootloader | UEFI (Secure Boot must be OFF) |
| **TPM 2.0 Support** | No (needs GPIO SPI add-on module) | **Yes** (Intel PTT / TPM 2.0 onboard) |
| **At-Rest Disk Encryption** | Standard LUKS (nodeID key) | **Hardware-sealed LUKS (TPM 2.0)** |
